Raycasting is a technique for drawing a convincing three-dimensional view of a world that is stored as a flat two-dimensional map. For every vertical strip of pixels on the screen, the renderer sends an imaginary line, a ray, out across the map until it meets a wall, measures how far it went, and paints a strip of wall whose height depends on that one distance. Nothing is rotated, nothing is projected, and no triangle is involved at any point.

It is the method behind Wolfenstein 3D (1992) and a long line of games after it. It became popular because it is fast on hardware with no floating-point unit worth speaking of, and it has stayed popular with people learning graphics because you can write the whole thing in an afternoon and understand every line of it afterwards.

This article builds such a renderer from an empty window, in steps that each end in something you can run and look at. Part I covers the classic engine: a grid, a camera, the grid walk, textured walls, and a textured floor and ceiling. Part II takes the one assumption that the classic engine never questions, that the floor is flat and every wall is the same height, and removes it, which turns out to buy steps, balconies, archways, open sky, roofs and hillsides for surprisingly little work. Part III is about speed, about the mistakes everyone makes, and about where to go afterwards.

You do not need any experience of 3D graphics. You need to be able to write a loop and an array, and to remember roughly what sine and cosine do. If you do not remember, section 3 is a refresher written for exactly that situation.

Part I · The classic engine

1What a raycaster actually draws

Start with what a raycaster is not, because that is where most of the confusion lives. It has no 3D model of anything. There are no triangles, no meshes, no camera matrices, and nothing anywhere in memory that could reasonably be called a room. What it has is a sheet of graph paper, an array of squares where some are wall and some are empty floor, plus one rule for turning a distance into a height on the screen.

Here is the rule, and it is the only piece of three-dimensional thinking in the whole idea: things that are further away look smaller. Twice as far, half as tall on screen. That is genuinely all of it. Hold your thumb up at arm's length and it covers a building; bring it closer and it covers the sky. Your eye is doing division.

Now stop thinking of your screen as a grid of pixels and start thinking of it as a row of narrow vertical strips, each one pixel wide. A window 320 pixels across is 320 strips standing side by side. We are going to fill in one strip at a time, left to right, and for each strip we ask three questions:

  • Looking out through this strip of the screen, which direction am I facing?
  • Walking across the graph paper in that direction, which wall square do I hit first?
  • How far away was it?

That last number is all we need. A near wall gets a tall stripe of colour, a far wall gets a short one, and both are centred on the middle of the screen. Paint the stripe, move on to the next one. Do that 320 times and the stripes standing side by side are the picture. The eye reads them as a room without being asked twice.

The imaginary line you walk along, out from your eye and through one strip of the screen, is the ray. Casting it, which is to say walking along it until it hits something, is where the name of the technique comes from. It is worth being honest about this early: we are not rendering in three dimensions. We are performing a convincing magic trick, and part of the pleasure of writing one is knowing exactly which part is the trick.

Why one column, and not one pixel

A fair question at this point is why the unit of work is a whole column rather than a single pixel. The answer is the reason raycasting is cheap enough to have worked on a 386.

Every wall in this world is perfectly vertical, and your camera never rolls sideways, so a vertical line in the world always appears as a vertical line on the screen. That means every pixel in a given column is looking at the same wall, at the same distance, at the same horizontal position along it. Whatever answer you work out for one pixel in the column is the answer for all of them. So you do the expensive part, the walk across the map, once per column, and then fill in a couple of hundred pixels with a loop that does almost nothing.

Ray tracing, by contrast, fires a ray per pixel and can therefore draw anything at all. It is also, at 1992 prices, approximately two hundred times more expensive. Raycasting is what you get when you notice that your world is made entirely of vertical walls and decide to be shameless about it.

And &thing means “pass this without copying it”, which you can ignore entirely.

Summary

The world is a 2D grid. The screen is a row of one-pixel-wide strips. For each strip we find the distance to the first wall along one ray, and draw a stripe whose height is proportional to 1 divided by that distance. Everything else in this article is bookkeeping on top of those two sentences.

2The world as a grid

Before any mathematics at all, get two dull things working: a grid you can ask questions about, and a rectangle of pixels you can colour in.

Your map is a drawing made of text. That is not a shortcut taken for the sake of a tutorial. Being able to see your level in your source file, and to edit it by typing, stays genuinely useful for a very long time.

// Anything off the edge counts as solid, so that a ray can never escape
// the array and run forever.
pub fn solid(x: i32, y: i32) -> bool {
    if x < 0 || y < 0 || x >= 16 || y >= 16 {
        return true;
    }
    MAP[y as usize].as_bytes()[x as usize] == b'#'
}

Two things about that function are worth noticing now rather than discovering later. It takes signed integers, because a ray heading north out of the top row will genuinely ask about row −1 and you would rather it got an answer than a crash. And it treats everything outside the map as solid, which quietly guarantees that every ray eventually stops. A wall of infinite thickness surrounds your world. Nobody has to know.

Whole numbers and the bits after the decimal point

Now one convention that matters far more than it looks, and which is worth writing on a sticky note.

Positions in this world are decimals, not whole numbers. You might be standing at (3.5, 10.5). Tile (x, y) is agreed to cover everything from x up to x + 1, and from y up to y + 1. From that single decision, three facts follow:

  • To find which tile a position is in, throw away the decimals. floor(3.5) = 3. Tiles are found by flooring, always.
  • (3.5, 10.5) is therefore the exact middle of tile (3, 10), not a corner of it. Corners are at whole numbers.
  • The decimal part on its own, 0.5 here, tells you how far across that tile you are, as a fraction from 0 to 1. This is a fact we lean on constantly from section 8 onwards, because it is exactly what a texture coordinate is.

Write that convention down and stick to it everywhere. A startling share of all raycaster bugs are two parts of the same program quietly disagreeing about it.

A rectangle of pixels

For the pixels, use the smallest thing that opens a window and lets you write colours into a plain array: SDL, SFML, raylib, an HTML canvas, or at the very beginning just writing the array out as an image file and opening it in a viewer. One 32-bit number per pixel, in the usual 0x00RRGGBB packing: two hexadecimal digits of red, two of green, two of blue, so 0xff0000 is red and 0xffffff is white. Whatever you choose, the shape of the program is this and never becomes more complicated than this:

Listing 2. The main loopthe entire structure of the program
let mut fb = vec![0u32; W * H];   // framebuffer: one u32 per pixel, 0x00RRGGBB

loop {
    handle_input(&mut cam);
    for x in 0..W {
        cast_column(x, &cam, &mut fb);   // <- the whole renderer lives in here
    }
    present(&fb);
}

W and H are the width and height of the window in pixels, and they will appear in nearly every listing from here on. fb is the framebuffer: a single flat array holding the whole image, in which the pixel at column x and row y lives at index y * W + x. Rows are stored one after another, so moving down one row means jumping forward by W. We will revisit that choice, and eventually reverse it, in section 16.

Checkpoint: stop here until this works

A window that fills with a solid colour, sixty times a second, and closes when you press Escape. Change the colour every frame so that you can see it really is redrawing. It looks like nothing, and it is nothing, but everything from here onwards is one function called inside that loop. Debugging graphics code is miserable if you are not completely certain that the window itself is fine.

Summary
  • The map is an array of characters; solid(x, y) answers questions about it and treats everything off the edge as wall.
  • World positions are decimals. Tile (x, y) spans x to x + 1. Floor a position to get its tile; the leftover fraction says where in the tile you are.
  • The renderer is one function called once per screen column, inside a loop that does nothing else.

3Coordinates, angles and vectors

A short refresher. If you are comfortable with unit vectors and radians, skim the diagram and carry on to section 4.

Everything from here on is arithmetic on positions and directions, and almost every baffling raycaster bug is really a disagreement about one of the three conventions below. Fortunately the computer is perfectly happy to do the boring trigonometry for us, so all we have to do is agree on what the numbers mean.

Which way is up

Our map has x increasing to the east and y increasing to the south. That second one catches people, because in school mathematics y goes up the page. Here it goes down, for the very practical reason that MAP[y][x] reads row y from the top of the array, and it is much less confusing to have the map look in memory the way it looks in the source file.

One consequence is worth stating before it surprises you: because y points down, angles increase clockwise when you look at the map from above. An angle of 0 faces east, and increasing it swings you round through south, west and north. This is self-consistent and everything works; it just is not the direction you may be expecting.

Angles, and why they are in radians

An angle here is a single number saying which way the player is facing, measured round from east. Every trigonometric function in every standard library measures angles in radians rather than degrees, so we will too, rather than converting back and forth and getting it wrong at three in the morning.

A radian is just a different sized unit for the same thing, in the way that a mile and a kilometre are both distances. One full turn is 2π radians rather than 360 degrees, which makes one radian about 57.3 degrees. To convert, multiply degrees by π/180.

Vectors, which are less alarming than they sound

A vector in this article is nothing more frightening than a pair of numbers used as an arrow: how far along x, how far along y. The pair (1, 0) is an arrow pointing east, one unit long. The pair (0.6, 0.8) is an arrow pointing east-south-east, and also happens to be one unit long, because 0.6 and 0.8 are two thirds of the famous 3-4-5 triangle.

Three operations are all we need, and you already know all three:

  • Scaling. Multiplying both numbers by k makes the arrow k times as long, pointing the same way. (1, 0) × 3 = (3, 0).
  • Adding. Adding two vectors componentwise means following one arrow and then the other. (1, 0) + (0, 2) = (1, 2), which is what you get by walking one step east and then two south.
  • Length. Pythagoras: the length of (x, y) is √(x² + y²). A vector of length exactly 1 is called a unit vector, and is the standard way of expressing “a direction, with no opinion about distance”.

The bridge between the angle and the vector is sine and cosine. If you stand at the centre of a circle of radius 1 and face at angle a, the point you are looking at is (cos a, sin a). That is the entire definition, and it is worth holding on to, because it means cos and sin are not abstractions here. They are literally “how far east” and “how far south” a unit arrow reaches.

If you prefer y upwards

You can have it. Flip the sign of y everywhere it is compared against the map, and swap the sign of the sideways vector in section 4 so that it still points to the player's right. The only rule that matters is that you pick one convention and never quietly change your mind halfway down a file. A raycaster in which half the code thinks north is up and the other half thinks it is down produces a world that renders mirrored, and you will lose an evening to it.

Summary
  • x increases east, y increases south, angles increase clockwise, and 0 faces east.
  • Angles are in radians. One full turn is 2π. The usual field of view, 66°, is about 1.15.
  • A vector is a pair of numbers used as an arrow. A unit vector has length 1. (cos a, sin a) is the unit vector at angle a.
  • Position plus direction times distance gives you the point you reach. That single expression is used in almost every section that follows.

4The camera and the rays

The one genuinely fiddly idea in Part I, and the choice that decides whether your walls come out straight or bowed like the side of a fish tank.

The camera is four numbers: where you are, which way you are facing, and how much of the world fits in the window.

Listing 3. The camerafour numbers, no matrices
pub struct Camera {
    pub x: f32,     // position, in tiles, decimals allowed
    pub y: f32,
    pub ang: f32,   // facing, in radians, 0 = east, increasing clockwise
    pub fov: f32,   // field of view, in radians; 1.15 is the classic value
}

From those we need to produce one ray per column: 320 slightly different directions, fanned out in front of the player. There is an obvious way to do that, and it is wrong, and it is worth understanding why before we do it properly, because the symptom is famous enough to have a name.

The tempting wrong way

The obvious approach is to fan the rays out at equal angles. Take your facing angle, subtract half the field of view, and add a little more for each column. It is one line of code and it produces something that almost works, which is the worst kind of wrong.

The trouble is that your screen is flat, and equal angles do not land at equal spacings on a flat surface. They bunch up in the middle of the screen and stretch out towards the edges. Point that camera at a flat wall and the wall bulges towards you in the centre of the image, like a shop window seen through a peephole. This is the classic fisheye distortion, and no amount of adjusting the field of view will remove it, because it is not a tuning problem. It is the wrong shape of projection.

What we want instead is what a real camera does: project onto a flat plane.

Two vectors instead of an angle

So we describe the camera with two vectors rather than one angle. Both come straight out of section 3.

  • dir is the way you are facing: a unit vector, so exactly one unit long. From your angle that is simply (cos ang, sin ang).
  • plane is an arrow at right angles to dir, pointing to your right. Picture an imaginary windscreen hanging one unit in front of your eye, at right angles to your gaze. plane is the arrow from the middle of that windscreen out to its right-hand edge.

To turn a vector 90 degrees clockwise in our coordinate system, swap the components and negate the first: (a, b) becomes (-b, a). Apply that to dir = (cos, sin) and you get (-sin, cos), which is the direction of plane. We will scale it in a moment.

That pair of vectors is called the camera plane, and the whole point of it is that a column of the screen is now a position along the windscreen rather than an angle. Give that position a number, camx, running from −1 at the far left of the window, through 0 dead ahead, to +1 at the far right:

camx = 2 · x / W − 1 ray  = dir + plane · camx
x
the column being drawn, from 0 on the left to W - 1 on the right.
W
the width of the window in pixels.
camx
where that column sits across the windscreen, as a fraction from −1 to +1.
ray
the direction to cast for this column: a vector, not an angle.

Check the first line against the ends: put x = 0 in and you get −1; put x = W in and you get +1. The middle column gives roughly 0. It is nothing more than a rescaling of the column number into a more convenient range.

Read the second line as an instruction rather than an equation: go one step forwards, then camx steps to the right. Where you end up is a point on the windscreen, and the arrow from your eye to that point is the ray for this column. Because those points are evenly spaced along a straight line, and not evenly spaced around a circle, a flat wall in front of you renders flat. That is exactly what a lens does, and it is why this formulation is worth the extra thirty seconds of thought.

The field of view is the length of the plane

How wide the fan spreads, the field of view, is set purely by how long you make plane. A longer sideways arrow reaches further across the windscreen, so more of the world is squeezed into the same window.

The exact relationship falls out of the right-angled triangle formed by the eye, the centre of the windscreen and its right-hand edge. The windscreen sits one unit ahead, and the half-width of it is the length of plane, so:

|plane| = tan(fov / 2)

The tangent of an angle is exactly the ratio of the opposite side to the adjacent side in a right-angled triangle, so this says nothing more exotic than “how far sideways do I reach after going one unit forwards”. For a 66 degree field of view, fov / 2 is 33 degrees, and tan(33°) is about 0.65. So plane is roughly two thirds the length of dir.

Notice that we are deliberately not making plane a unit vector. Its length carries meaning. Hold on to that fact, because in section 5 it pays a second dividend that looks very much like luck and is not.

Listing 4. Setting up the cameraonce per frame, not once per column
let (sa, ca) = cam.ang.sin_cos();
let dirx = ca;                    // dir = (cos ang, sin ang), length 1
let diry = sa;

// plane is dir turned 90 degrees clockwise, then scaled. It is *not*
// normalised: its length is what sets the field of view.
let plane_len = (cam.fov * 0.5).tan();
let planex = -sa * plane_len;
let planey =  ca * plane_len;

// Focal length in pixels: how far in front of the eye the screen would
// have to sit for a W-pixel-wide window to cover this field of view.
let focal = (W as f32 * 0.5) / (cam.fov * 0.5).tan();

Three of those deserve a sentence each.

The field of view is in radians, and about 1.15, which is 66 degrees, is the classic value. Go much wider and the edges of the screen begin to look stretched. That is a real and unavoidable property of flat projection rather than a bug in your code, and it is the same reason a very wide-angle photograph makes people at the edge of the frame look like they have been sat on.

The focal length is the same quantity as the plane length, measured in pixels instead of world units. If the windscreen is W pixels wide and reaches tan(fov/2) sideways for every one unit forwards, then to be W/2 pixels wide on each side it must sit (W/2) / tan(fov/2) pixels in front of the eye. That is the number above. It is the only quantity the projection needs, and from section 6 onwards very nearly everything gets multiplied by it.

sin_cos returns both at once, because the two are calculated together anyway and it is free. Small mercies.

Summary
  • Fanning rays out at equal angles gives fisheye distortion. Spacing them evenly along a flat plane does not.
  • dir is the unit vector you are facing. plane is at right angles to it, pointing right, with length tan(fov/2).
  • Column x maps to camx = 2x/W - 1, and its ray is dir + plane × camx.
  • focal = (W/2) / tan(fov/2) is the screen distance in pixels, and it is the only number the projection needs.

5Walking the grid: DDA

Now the walk itself: from your position, along the ray, one grid square at a time, until something stops you. This is where the technique earns its lunch.

We have a starting point and a direction. We need to know which wall the ray meets first, and how far away it is. The whole of the rest of the renderer is downstream of that one answer.

The lazy way, and why it lets you down

The obvious approach is to creep along the ray in small steps. Move a twentieth of a tile, check whether you are standing in a wall, repeat until you are. It works, it is four lines long, and you should feel free to write it first if it helps you see the shape of the problem. It has two faults, though, and they are the two faults you least want.

It is slow, because the overwhelming majority of those steps land in the same square you were already in and tell you nothing you did not already know. Finding your way across a city by taking a measurement every millimetre will get you there, but you will want a packed lunch.

It is also wrong, occasionally and unpredictably. Make the step large enough to be tolerable and the ray can clip the corner of a wall block and carry on into the room beyond, so a pillar develops a hole you can see through from one specific spot. Make it small enough to be safe and you are back to being slow. There is no setting that is both.

What we want is a method that visits every square the ray passes through, in order, never skipping one and never testing the same square twice. That method is the digital differential analyser, universally shortened to DDA. The name is inherited from old line-drawing hardware and tells you nothing useful, so ignore it and look at the idea.

The idea: only boundaries matter

A ray only ever leaves a square by crossing a grid line, and grid lines come in exactly two kinds: the vertical ones, at whole-numbered values of x, and the horizontal ones, at whole numbered values of y. Nothing interesting can happen between crossings. So instead of stepping a fixed small distance, we jump from boundary to boundary, and examine only the cells the ray genuinely enters.

Here is the observation that makes it cheap. Crossings of the same kind are evenly spaced. If a ray drifts slowly rightwards, it might cross a vertical grid line every four units of travel, and it will then keep doing exactly that, forever, every four units. The same is true of the horizontal ones, at their own separate interval. Neither interval ever changes, because the ray never turns.

So the walk becomes a race between two counters, each ticking along at its own fixed interval, and at every round we advance whichever one is behind.

Step 1: which cell are we starting in

Floor the camera position, exactly as promised in section 2:

Listing 5. The starting cell
let mut mapx = cam.x.floor() as i32;
let mut mapy = cam.y.floor() as i32;

mapx and mapy are the integer coordinates of the cell we are currently inside. They are the only pieces of state the walk really carries: as it proceeds, they will step to the neighbouring cell, one axis at a time, and the walk ends when they land on something solid.

Step 2: the two intervals, or delta distance

Suppose the ray direction is (rx, ry). Travel a distance t along it and you have moved t · rx in x and t · ry in y. To move a full 1 unit in x, which is what crossing from one column of the grid to the next requires, you therefore need t = 1 / rx. Taking the absolute value, because a ray heading west covers ground just as fast as one heading east:

ddx = |1 / rx| travel between two consecutive vertical grid lines ddy = |1 / ry| travel between two consecutive horizontal grid lines

These are conventionally called the delta distances, delta being the usual word for “the gap between one and the next”. They depend only on the ray direction, so they are computed once, before the walk starts, and never touched again.

A sanity check is worth doing here, because these two are easy to get upside down. A ray pointing almost due east has a large rx (close to 1) and a tiny ry. It crosses vertical lines constantly, so ddx should be small: and indeed 1/1 is 1. It hardly ever crosses a horizontal one, so ddy should be huge: and 1 / 0.001 is 1000. Good.

That last case also shows the trap. A ray pointing exactly east has ry = 0 and the division blows up. We handle it by declaring the next horizontal crossing to be unimaginably far away, which is not a fudge but simply the truth: it never happens.

Step 3: the distance to the first crossing, or side distance

The delta distances tell us how far apart the crossings are, but not where the first one is, and the first one depends on where in the cell we happen to be standing. Call these two the side distances, sdx and sdy: the distance from the camera to the next vertical grid line, and to the next horizontal one.

There are two cases per axis, depending on which way the ray points:

  • If rx is positive, the ray is heading east, so the next vertical line is the right-hand edge of this cell, at mapx + 1. The remaining distance across the cell is mapx + 1 - cam.x, as a fraction of a whole cell, so multiply that fraction by ddx to turn it into travel along the ray.
  • If rx is negative, the ray is heading west and the next line is the left-hand edge, at mapx. The fraction is cam.x - mapx.

The same reasoning applies to y with the roles of north and south. While we are here we also record stepx and stepy, each either +1 or −1, saying which direction to move mapx and mapy when we do cross.

Step 4: the march

Everything is now in place, and the loop itself is almost embarrassingly short. Each round: compare the two side distances; the smaller one is the nearer boundary, so that is the one we meet next. Cross it, step the cell index that way, and add that counter's delta back on so it now points at the crossing after that. Then look at the cell we have arrived in. If it is solid, we are done.

It is much easier to believe all this once you have watched it happen, so here is the same march with the handbrake on. Drag inside the grid to aim the ray, then press Step to advance one round at a time and watch the two counters take turns:

Listing 6. Casting one columnthe heart of the whole engine
pub fn cast_column(x: usize, cam: &Camera) -> (f32, u8, i32, i32) {
    // 1. the ray for this column (section 4)
    let camx = 2.0 * x as f32 / W as f32 - 1.0;
    let rx = dirx + planex * camx;
    let ry = diry + planey * camx;

    // 2. which cell we start in
    let mut mapx = cam.x.floor() as i32;
    let mut mapy = cam.y.floor() as i32;

    // 3. distance between successive crossings of each kind
    let ddx = if rx.abs() < 1e-8 { 1e30 } else { (1.0 / rx).abs() };
    let ddy = if ry.abs() < 1e-8 { 1e30 } else { (1.0 / ry).abs() };

    // 4. distance to the *first* crossing, and which way we walk
    let (stepx, mut sdx) = if rx < 0.0 {
        (-1, (cam.x - mapx as f32) * ddx)          // heading west
    } else {
        (1, (mapx as f32 + 1.0 - cam.x) * ddx)     // heading east
    };
    let (stepy, mut sdy) = if ry < 0.0 {
        (-1, (cam.y - mapy as f32) * ddy)          // heading north
    } else {
        (1, (mapy as f32 + 1.0 - cam.y) * ddy)     // heading south
    };

    // 5. march
    for _ in 0..160 {
        let side = if sdx < sdy { 0u8 } else { 1u8 };
        let dist = if side == 0 { sdx } else { sdy };

        if side == 0 { sdx += ddx; mapx += stepx; }
        else         { sdy += ddy; mapy += stepy; }

        if solid(mapx, mapy) {
            return (dist, side, mapx, mapy);
        }
    }
    (34.0, 0, mapx, mapy)   // nothing found within the far clip
}

Four details in that listing are worth pointing at directly.

The 1e30 guard is the axis-aligned case from earlier. A ray pointing exactly along an axis never crosses grid lines of the other kind at all, so we say the next one is unimaginably far away and the race is settled by the other counter every single round. This is one of those situations where the special case handles itself if you let it.

The order inside the loop is deliberate. We record the distance to the boundary before crossing it, then cross, then test. So dist is always the distance to the face of the cell we are entering, which is exactly the surface we can see. Get this the wrong way round and every wall sits one cell too far away, which looks almost right and is maddening to spot.

side records which kind of boundary we came through: 0 for a vertical line, meaning we are looking at an east or west face of the block, and 1 for a horizontal one, meaning a north or south face. Keep it. You will want it twice more, for shading in section 6 and for texture coordinates in section 8.

The iteration cap of 160 is a seatbelt. In principle the border of solid tiles round the map guarantees the loop ends, but a bug elsewhere, or a floating-point value that has gone to infinity, should not be able to hang your program. It also doubles as a far clipping distance: anything beyond it is simply declared to be nothing at all.

Why it cannot go wrong

It is worth convincing yourself, rather than taking it on trust, that this really does visit every cell in order. Two facts do it.

First, the ray can only leave a cell through a boundary, and every boundary is either vertical or horizontal, so the next event is always one of the two we are tracking. There is no third possibility. Second, the two counters always hold the distance to the next crossing of their kind, so the smaller of them is the next crossing of any kind. Taking the minimum each round therefore produces the crossings in strictly increasing order of distance, with none omitted.

The one case that makes people nervous is the exact diagonal, where the ray passes precisely through a lattice corner and sdx == sdy. The comparison picks one arbitrarily, the walk steps through the cell on that side, and the following round immediately steps through the other. No cell is skipped, and the picture is correct. Floating-point equality is not something to be frightened of here.

The distance you get is already the right one

Remember the fisheye from section 4? It has a second half, and this is where we collect the reward for not normalising the camera plane.

Even with a flat windscreen, a ray heading off to one side has physically further to travel before it reaches the same wall than a ray heading straight ahead. If you use that travelled distance to size your wall stripes, the wall bulges towards you all over again, and you have reinvented the fisheye by the back door. What you want instead is the perpendicular distance: not “how far did I walk” but “how far ahead of me is it”, which is the length of the wall's shadow on the direction you are facing.

Now look closely at what dist actually measures. Our ray vector is dir + plane × camx, and the loop measures distance in units of that vector's length, because ddx = |1/rx| was derived from “how many ray vectors get me one unit across in x”. So one unit of dist means one whole ray vector travelled.

And ray is made of exactly two pieces: one dir, which is one unit of pure forwards, plus some multiple of plane, which is entirely sideways because it is at right angles to dir. Sideways movement contributes nothing whatsoever to how far ahead of you something is. So every unit of dist is precisely one unit forwards, regardless of which column we are drawing:

dist × ray = dist × dir one unit forwards per unit of dist + dist × camx × plane purely sideways: no depth at all

The number falling out of the loop is therefore already the perpendicular distance. No cosine, no correction factor, no square root, and nothing to remember to apply. It is not luck. It is the reason the camera was set up that way in the first place, and it is the single neatest thing about this formulation.

If you inherited a different formulation

Plenty of raycasters build their rays from an angle per column and then multiply the resulting distance by cos(ray_angle - facing_angle) to straighten the wall out again. That is the same correction applied by hand afterwards, and it gives an identical picture. If you are reading such a tutorial alongside this one, that cosine is the thing our camera plane has already absorbed. Do not apply it twice, or your walls will curve the other way, which is at least a novel bug.

  • Stop when the cell you step into is solid. The distance you already have is the perpendicular distance, which is exactly what the next section wants.

6Drawing a wall

One division turns that distance into a stripe of pixels, and suddenly there is a room on the screen.

This is where the promise from section 1 is cashed in: further away means smaller. Precisely, a wall one world unit tall, seen at perpendicular distance d, covers this many pixels of screen height:

line_h = focal × Hwall / d
line_h
the height of the stripe to draw, in pixels.
focal
the focal length in pixels, from section 4. Constant for the whole frame.
Hwall
the height of the wall in world units. For now it is 1, so it disappears from the code.
d
the perpendicular distance from section 5.

Double the distance and the stripe covers half as many pixels, which is the whole of our three-dimensional theory in one line.

Where that formula comes from

You can take it on faith, but it is genuinely a three-line argument and knowing it makes the next few sections much easier, because we keep reusing the same triangle.

Look at Figure 9. Your eye, the top of the wall, and the point where that sightline crosses the windscreen form a triangle. Your eye, the top of the wall, and the base of the wall form a larger triangle with exactly the same shape, because they share the same corner at your eye and both have a vertical opposite side. Triangles with the same shape but different sizes have all their sides in the same ratio. So the height of the stripe on the screen is to the height of the wall in the world as the focal length is to the distance. Rearranged, that is the equation above.

No trigonometry is involved anywhere in it. It is the same rule that makes your thumb cover a distant building, and it will do all our projection work for the rest of the article.

Turning that into pixels

The stripe is centred vertically on the middle of the window, because for the moment your eye is exactly halfway up the wall and looking level. Half the stripe goes above the middle row and half below.

Listing 7. From one distance to one stripethe payoff
let (dist, side, mapx, mapy) = cast_column(x, cam);

let line_h = (focal / dist) as i32;           // walls are 1 unit tall
let mid    = H as i32 / 2;
let top    = mid - line_h / 2;                // unclamped: keep it, section 8 needs it
let y0     = top.clamp(0, H as i32) as usize;
let y1     = (mid + line_h / 2).clamp(0, H as i32) as usize;

// Flat shading for now. The two wall orientations get different
// brightness, which is what makes corners readable.
let base: u32 = if side == 0 { 0x9aa8bb } else { 0x6f7d8f };

for y in 0..y0   { fb[y * W + x] = 0x151b26; }   // ceiling
for y in y0..y1  { fb[y * W + x] = base;     }   // wall
for y in y1..H   { fb[y * W + x] = 0x272f3c; }   // floor

The clamping deserves a warning, because it catches absolutely everybody once. Walk right up to a wall and dist becomes tiny, so line_h becomes enormous: tens of thousands of pixels for a window a few hundred tall. Write those rows into your array without clamping and you will scribble far past the end of it and crash. Or, worse, not crash.

Notice as well that the clamp happens after the midpoint arithmetic, and that top is kept in its raw, unclamped form. That is not fussiness. It keeps the stripe centred as it grows rather than sliding upwards, and section 8 needs the original unclamped top to work out which part of a texture the visible portion corresponds to.

The two shades of grey are the cheapest lighting trick in the business. Faces entered through a vertical boundary get one brightness and faces entered through a horizontal boundary get another, as though a light hung somewhere to the north. It is complete fiction, it costs a single comparison, and without it a room of identically coloured walls is very hard to read, because corners simply vanish.

That is the entire Part I renderer. Here it is assembled, so that you can see how little there actually is:

pub fn render(cam: &Camera, fb: &mut [u32]) {

    let (sa, ca) = cam.ang.sin_cos();
    let (dirx, diry) = (ca, sa);
    let plane_len = (cam.fov * 0.5).tan();

        let (dist, side, _mx, _my) = cast_column(x, cam);

Checkpoint: the big one

You now have exactly what the interactive panel at the top of this page draws: walls, corners, corridors and a real sense of depth, out of no 3D library and no matrices. Congratulations, you have just reinvented a small but important part of Wolfenstein 3D. Everything in the rest of this article is refinement of this picture.

If it comes out wrong, the failures are usefully distinctive, and section 17 has a longer table of them. The four to know now: walls that bulge outwards in the middle mean you are measuring the wrong distance; a view that swims or shears as you turn usually means plane is not at right angles to dir; stripes in the wrong order mean camx runs the wrong way, so flip its sign; and a picture that is entirely one colour usually means the ray started inside a wall.

Summary
  • line_h = focal / d for a wall one unit tall. It comes from similar triangles, not from trigonometry.
  • Centre the stripe on the middle row, then clamp to the window. Clamp after centring, and keep the unclamped top.
  • Shade the two wall orientations differently or your corners will be invisible.

7Moving around

Walking is two lines of arithmetic. Not walking through walls is the part worth doing carefully.

Moving forwards means adding a little bit of dir to your position. Moving sideways means adding a little bit of plane, which conveniently already points to your right. Both vectors are lying around from section 4, so movement needs no new mathematics at all.

Multiply by the time elapsed since the last frame, dt in seconds, so that your speed is expressed in world units per second and does not change when the frame rate does. A player who walks twice as fast on a faster machine is a classic and thoroughly avoidable bug.

Listing 9. Inputone key, one line
let speed = 3.0 * dt;      // 3 world units per second, times frame time
let turn  = 2.2 * dt;      // 2.2 radians per second, about 126 degrees

if key(W) { try_move(cam,  dirx * speed,  diry * speed); }
if key(S) { try_move(cam, -dirx * speed, -diry * speed); }
// Strafing walks along the camera plane instead of the direction. The
// plane points to your *right*, since it runs from the centre of the
// screen towards its right-hand edge, so A is the negative one.
if key(A) { try_move(cam, -planex * speed, -planey * speed); }
if key(D) { try_move(cam,  planex * speed,  planey * speed); }
if key(Left)  { cam.ang -= turn; }
if key(Right) { cam.ang += turn; }

One small oddity: because plane is tan(fov/2) long rather than one unit long, strafing is slightly slower than walking forwards, and gets slower still if you narrow the field of view. If that bothers you, divide by plane_len. It has never bothered anybody enough to be worth the division.

Collision, or how not to feel like a shopping trolley

Testing “is the square I am about to stand in solid?” technically works and feels awful. Two rules fix it, and both are about how movement feels rather than about correctness:

  • Move each axis separately. Test x + dx against the old y, then y + dy against the new x. If you test the combined position instead, you stop dead the instant you brush a wall at an angle. Testing separately lets the blocked axis fail while the free one carries on, so you slide along the wall rather than sticking to it.
  • Give the player a radius. Check the tile at x + dx + r × sign(dx), with r around 0.25, so that you stop a little short of the wall rather than with your nose against it. Without this the camera gets close enough that the near wall fills the entire screen, and close enough that floating-point rounding can put your eye on the wrong side of it.
Listing 10. Collisionslide along walls, do not stick to them
fn try_move(cam: &mut Camera, dx: f32, dy: f32) {
    const R: f32 = 0.25;                       // the player's radius, in tiles

    // x first, against the *old* y
    let nx = cam.x + dx + R * dx.signum();
    if !solid(nx as i32, cam.y as i32) { cam.x += dx; }

    // then y, against the *new* x
    let ny = cam.y + dy + R * dy.signum();
    if !solid(cam.x as i32, ny as i32) { cam.y += dy; }
}

Mouse look is the arrow keys with a different source: accumulate the relative mouse motion in x into cam.ang. Multiply the keys by frame time but not the mouse. Mouse deltas are already per-frame quantities, so scaling them by dt as well makes your sensitivity change with your frame rate, which feels haunted.

Summary
  • Forwards is dir, sideways is plane. Scale both by dt.
  • Resolve x and y separately so the player slides along walls.
  • Test a point a small radius ahead of the player, not the player's exact position.

8Textures

A stripe of flat colour becomes a stripe of stone once you know two things: which part of the wall you hit, and how fast to walk down the picture as you paint.

A texture is just a small square image held in memory as an array of colours, and one dot of it is a texel. The separate word is worth having, because a texel is not a pixel: depending on how far away the wall is, one screen pixel may end up covering eight texels or one ninth of one.

Make the side length TS a power of two, because 64 is plenty and because it lets you wrap coordinates around with a cheap bitwise & instead of a modulo. Where the images come from does not matter in the slightest: load PNGs, generate them from noise at start-up, or type a few in by hand.

To paint a stripe you need two coordinates, and they are conventionally called u and v:

  • u says how far along the wall face you hit, from one edge of the block to the other.
  • v says how far down the wall each screen row is.

Both run from 0 to 1 across the texture, and both then get multiplied by TS to become an index into the array.

Across the wall: the u coordinate

We know how far the ray travelled, and we know its direction, so the point where it hit is just position plus direction times distance. Once we have that point, u is the fractional part of whichever coordinate runs along the face we hit, and this is precisely the convention from section 2 earning its keep.

Which coordinate is that? If we entered through a vertical boundary (side == 0) then the face we are looking at is a north-south face, so moving along it changes y, and u comes from the fraction of hy. If we came through a horizontal boundary it is the other way round.

Listing 11. Across the wallthe fractional part is the answer
let hx = cam.x + rx * dist;      // where the ray actually hit, in world units
let hy = cam.y + ry * dist;

let mut u = if side == 0 { hy - hy.floor() } else { hx - hx.floor() };

// Faces on opposite sides of a block are seen from opposite directions, so
// one of each pair has to be flipped or the texture reads mirrored.
if (side == 0 && stepx > 0) || (side == 1 && stepy < 0) {
    u = 1.0 - u;
}
let tu = ((u * TS as f32) as usize) & (TS - 1);

That flip is worth a moment, because it looks arbitrary and is not. Walk round a pillar with writing on it. The east face and the west face are both read left to right by you, but they run in opposite directions in world coordinates, because you turned around to look at the second one. Without the flip, half the walls in your level display their texture as though held up to a mirror. With numbered or lettered textures the effect is immediately obvious; with stonework you will not notice for weeks and then never be able to unsee it.

Down the wall: the v coordinate

The stripe is line_h pixels tall and the texture is TS texels tall, so each screen row we move down corresponds to TS / line_h texels down the texture. If the stripe is 400 pixels tall and the texture 64 texels, each row advances 0.16 of a texel, so several screen rows share one texel and the texture looks chunky. That is not a defect, it is 1992.

The subtlety is where to start. When you are close to a wall the stripe is taller than the window, so the top of it is off the screen, and the first row you actually draw is already some way down the texture. That is why section 6 insisted on keeping the unclamped top: the difference between where the stripe really begins and where drawing really begins is exactly how many texels to skip.

Listing 12. Down the wallthe inner loop of the whole renderer
let step = TS as f32 / line_h as f32;   // texels per screen row

// `top` is the *unclamped* top of the stripe, from listing 7. If the stripe
// starts above the window, (y0 - top) is how many rows were cut off.
let mut tv = (y0 as i32 - top) as f32 * step;

for y in y0..y1 {
    let texel = tex[tu * TS + (tv as usize & (TS - 1))];
    fb[y * W + x] = shade(texel, side, dist);
    tv += step;
}
A one-character speed-up

Look closely at the lookup in listing 12: tex[tu * TS + tv]. The column comes first. Most people store images row by row, which would be tex[tv * TS + tu].

Storing them column by column instead means that painting one wall stripe reads one unbroken run of memory, rather than leaping TS texels forward for every single pixel. Memory read in order arrives from the cache; memory read in leaps arrives from a good deal further away. It is a one-character change and you can measure the difference. This is the first appearance of a theme that section 16 is entirely made of: in this renderer the vertical strip is the unit of work, so anything you can arrange to be contiguous down a strip, should be.

Shading

Finally, distance shading. Split the texel colour into its red, green and blue bytes, multiply all three by a factor that shrinks as dist grows, and reassemble them. Distant walls go dark, which the eye reads as depth for free. Then darken every side == 1 face by a fixed amount, exactly as in section 6.

That second trick is pure fakery, costs one multiply, and nothing else this cheap does anywhere near as much to make a room legible. Section 15 replaces both with something considerably better, but you will find you keep the distance falloff anyway.

Summary
  • u, across the wall, is the fractional part of the hit coordinate that runs along the face. Flip it on two of the four face orientations.
  • v, down the wall, advances by TS / line_h per screen row, starting at an offset derived from the unclamped top of the stripe.
  • Store textures column-major. The whole engine reads vertically.

9Floors and ceilings

Walls were “walk outwards until you hit something”. Floors are the other way round: for every row of the screen, work out how far away the ground you can see there must be.

Start with the horizon: the row of the screen where the floor disappears into the distance. While you are looking straight ahead it is simply the middle row of the window. Everything below it is floor, everything above it is ceiling, and the further a row sits from the horizon, the closer the ground it is showing.

If that sounds backwards, look down at your own feet. They are at the very bottom of your vision, and they are the nearest floor you can see. The floor at the far end of the room is nearly at eye level. Distance and rows-from-the-horizon run in opposite directions, and they do so as a reciprocal, which is to say a division.

That relationship is the whole of floor casting, and it is the same similar-triangles argument as section 6, turned on its side. For a row y below the horizon, the ground visible there is at perpendicular distance:

d = (eye_height − floor_height) × focal / (y − horizon)
eye_height
how high the camera is above the world's zero level. In Part I this is a fixed 0.5, halfway up a wall.
floor_height
how high the floor is. In Part I this is always 0, so the whole numerator is just the eye height. Section 11 is about what happens when it is not.
focal
the focal length in pixels from section 4, exactly as before.
y − horizon
how many rows below the horizon this row is. One row below the horizon looks at ground very far away; two hundred rows below it looks at ground almost under your feet.

Once you have d, finding the actual spot on the floor that the pixel is showing is the same expression as always: start at the camera and travel d along this column's ray. That gives you a world position such as (7.32, 4.85), and now the convention from section 2 pays for itself twice in one line. The whole-number part, (7, 4), tells you which tile you are looking at and therefore which texture to use. The fractional part, (0.32, 0.85), is how far across that tile you are, and is therefore your texture coordinate. No extra work of any kind.

Listing 13. Floor castingwalking down one column
for y in (horizon + 1)..H as i32 {
    let d = (cam.z - floor_h) * focal / (y - horizon) as f32;

    let wx = cam.x + rx * d;      // the world point this pixel shows
    let wy = cam.y + ry * d;

    let tex = floor_tex(wx as i32, wy as i32);           // whole part: which tile
    let tu  = ((wx - wx.floor()) * TS as f32) as usize;  // fraction: where in it
    let tv  = ((wy - wy.floor()) * TS as f32) as usize;

    fb[y as usize * W + x] = shade(tex[tu * TS + tv], 2, d);
}

The ceiling is the same equation with the sign flipped, for rows above the horizon: (ceiling_h - eye) * focal / (horizon - y). Both denominators count rows away from the horizon, so both are positive, and both blow up as you approach it. Start your loops one row clear of the horizon and the problem never arises.

A fork in the road: per column, or per row?

Most tutorials cast floors one horizontal row at a time rather than one column at a time. The reason is a good one: every pixel in a given row is at the same distance, so you can work out the world position at the two ends of the row and then walk between them with a couple of additions per pixel, instead of a division. It is meaningfully faster, and if all you want is a flat floor you should do it that way.

But it quietly assumes that the entire row of floor is at the same height, and that assumption is exactly what Part II of this article spends its time dismantling. Doing it per column, at one division per pixel, is what will let a step, a balcony, a bridge and a hillside all appear in the same picture. Take the slower road here. You will get the speed back in section 16, when the division becomes a table lookup.

Checkpoint: you have built Wolfenstein 3D

Textured walls, a textured floor and ceiling, mouse look, and collision that slides. That is a complete engine of the kind that shipped in 1992 and sold rather well. If you stop here you have not stopped early.

Everything in Part II comes from questioning a single assumption which is currently buried everywhere in what you have just written: that the floor is at height 0, the ceiling is at height 1, and every wall runs from one to the other.

Summary
  • The horizon is the row at eye level. Distance to the floor is inversely proportional to how many rows below it you are.
  • d = eye_height × focal / (y - horizon) for a floor at height 0.
  • Travel d along the column's ray to get the world position; its whole part picks the tile, its fractional part is the texture coordinate.

10Eye height and looking up

Two small changes, neither of which looks like much, which everything in the rest of the article leans on.

So far your eye has been fixed at height 0.5, halfway up a wall, and the horizon has been fixed at the middle row of the window. Give the camera a height of its own, z, and let the horizon move.

Listing 14. Looking up and downa shear, not a rotation
// Pitch shifts the horizon up and down the screen. It is not a rotation:
// real pitch would need a second axis in the cast.
let horizon = ((H as f32 * 0.5) + cam.pitch * focal).round() as i32;

That deserves unpacking, because it is a cheat and it is worth knowing which kind.

Properly tilting the camera upwards would mean rotating the rays in three dimensions, and the entire engine so far assumes that rays are flat arrows on a flat map. So we do not rotate anything. We simply slide the whole picture up or down by moving the row we call the horizon, which is a shear rather than a rotation. Straight vertical lines stay straight and vertical, which is exactly what our one-ray-per-column trick requires, and the price is a mild stretching that becomes noticeable somewhere around 35 degrees from level. This is what every engine of the era did, and if you have ever thought that looking up in an old first-person game felt slightly like peering through a letterbox, this is why.

Rounding the horizon to a whole row looks fussy and pays off twice. The floor equation divides by y - horizon; if that is always a whole number, the division can be replaced by a lookup in a small table, which section 16 will do. And surfaces that meet at the horizon then meet exactly, rather than leaving a half-pixel seam you can see through.

One projection function, used by everything

Now the second change, which is really a piece of housekeeping and is the single most useful discipline in this article. Everything from here on has to answer the same question over and over: something is at world height h and distance d; which row of the screen is it on?

Write that down once, and then never work it out any other way again.

Listing 15. The one ruleevery surface in the world goes through this
/// First screen row at or below world height `hgt`, seen at distance `d`.
pub fn row_at(hgt: f32, d: f32, f: &Frame) -> i32 {
    (f.horizon as f32 - (hgt - f.pz) * f.focal / d).ceil() as i32
}
hgt
the world height we are asking about: the top of a wall, the level of a floor, the underside of an arch.
f.pz
the height of the player's eye. Subtracting it gives the height relative to the eye, which is what the projection actually cares about. Something at exactly eye height gives zero, and lands on the horizon row.
f.focal / d
the same “further is smaller” scaling as section 6: how many pixels one world unit covers at this distance.
.ceil()
rounds towards the bottom of the screen, so that a span always starts at the first row genuinely at or below the height asked for. Which way you round matters less than that everybody rounds the same way.

It is the same projection you already have, rearranged. Instead of “how tall is this on screen” it answers “which row is this height on”.

Use it for everything from here on: the top of a wall, the bottom of a wall, where a floor span starts, where a ceiling span ends. That discipline is not tidiness. The moment two surfaces meet in the world, they have to meet on the same pixel row on the screen, and the surest way to guarantee that is for both of them to have asked the same function the same question. Compute one of them by a slightly different route, even an algebraically identical one, and floating point will eventually disagree with itself by one pixel. The symptom is a single-pixel crack along every step in your world that flickers as you walk. It is a genuinely horrible bug to find after the fact, and free to prevent now.

Summary
  • The camera gains a height z. Looking up and down moves the horizon row, which is a shear and not a real rotation.
  • Round the horizon to a whole row.
  • row_at(height, distance) converts a world height into a screen row. Route every surface in the renderer through it, without exception.

11The clip window

The one big idea in this article. Textbook raycasting stops at the first solid tile. If you keep walking instead, you get steps, balconies, arches and open sky, and it is still the same loop.

Up to now a tile has been a yes-or-no question: wall, or not wall. Replace that with two numbers, how high the floor is and how high the ceiling is:

Listing 16. A richer tiletwo numbers instead of one flag
pub struct Tile {
    pub fh: f32,     // floor height
    pub ch: f32,     // ceiling height
    pub ftex: u8,    // which texture for the floor, the ceiling, the wall face
    pub ctex: u8,
    pub wtex: u8,
    pub flags: u8,
}

// "Solid" stops being a special case. It is simply a tile whose ceiling
// is not above its floor: there is no room to stand in it.
impl Tile {
    pub fn solid(&self) -> bool { self.ch <= self.fh }
}

Look at what that buys immediately, before a single line of the renderer has changed. A tile whose floor is at 0.4 is a step you walk up. A tile whose ceiling is at 1.2 is a low arch you duck through. A tile whose floor is at 3.0 is a wall when seen from below and a balcony when seen from above, depending entirely on where you are standing. And an ordinary solid wall is just a tile with no gap between its floor and its ceiling.

Giving the column a memory

To draw all that, the ray can no longer stop at the first obstruction, because there may be more world visible above or below it. So we let it keep walking, and give the column a memory: a clip window, a pair of row numbers ytop and ybot marking the band of this column that has not been painted yet.

It starts as the entire height of the window, and it only ever gets narrower. As the ray marches outwards, each cell paints into it and squeezes it from one end or the other. When the two ends meet, this column is finished and we stop walking, because there can be nothing else to see: every row already has something nearer in front of it.

At every cell along the way, the loop does three things:

  1. Paint this tile's floor into the bottom of the window and its ceiling into the top, out as far as the cell boundary.
  2. Compare heights with the next tile. A higher floor exposes a wall face standing up from the ground, which we will call a riser. A lower ceiling exposes one hanging down, which is a lintel or the front of an arch.
  3. If the next tile is solid, fill whatever is left of the window with wall and stop.

Here is that loop running. The room below has a staircase, a balcony at the top of it, a sunken pit and a low arch, and none of those are things the renderer knows about: every one of them is two numbers on a tile. The strip along the bottom is the cross-section along the ray you are currently looking down, which is to say, it is what Figure 12 would look like if it were drawn from your point of view rather than by hand.

Listing 17. The extended marchlisting 6's loop, with two integers added
let mut ytop = 0i32;              // the unpainted band: [ytop, ybot)
let mut ybot = H as i32;
let mut cur  = tile_at(mapx, mapy);

loop {
    let side = if sdx < sdy { 0u8 } else { 1u8 };
    let dfar = if side == 0 { sdx } else { sdy };   // far edge of this cell

    // --- 1. the floor and ceiling of the tile we are inside -----------
    if cam.z > cur.fh {
        let ybeg = row_at(cur.fh, dfar, f).max(ytop).max(horizon + 1);
        if ybeg < ybot { sink.floor(ybeg, ybot, &cur); }
        ybot = ybot.min(ybeg);
    }
    if cur.ch > cam.z {
        let yend = row_at(cur.ch, dfar, f).min(ybot).min(horizon);
        if ytop < yend { sink.ceiling(ytop, yend, &cur); }
        ytop = ytop.max(yend);
    }
    if ytop >= ybot { break; }        // column finished, nothing else is visible

    // --- 2. cross into the next cell ---------------------------------
    if side == 0 { sdx += ddx; mapx += stepx; }
    else         { sdy += ddy; mapy += stepy; }
    let next = tile_at(mapx, mapy);

    // --- 3. what the height difference exposes ------------------------
    if next.solid() {
        sink.wall(ytop, ybot, dfar, &next);
        break;
    }
    if next.fh > cur.fh {                            // step up: a riser
        let ybeg = row_at(next.fh, dfar, f).max(ytop);
        if ybeg < ybot { sink.wall(ybeg, ybot, dfar, &next); }
        ybot = ybot.min(ybeg);
    }
    if next.ch < cur.ch {                            // lower roof: a lintel
        let yend = row_at(next.ch, dfar, f).min(ybot);
        if ytop < yend { sink.wall(ytop, yend, dfar, &next); }
        ytop = ytop.max(yend);
    }

    if ytop >= ybot { break; }
    cur = next;
}

The two conditions in part 1 are worth reading carefully, because they are the only place the renderer ever asks where your eye is. You can see a tile's floor only if your eye is above it, and its ceiling only if your eye is below it. Stand on a balcony and you see the floor of the courtyard; stand in the courtyard and you do not see the balcony's floor, you see the underside of it, which is the ceiling of the tile below. Two comparisons, and the renderer never needs to be told which is which.

Forty lines. That loop is every staircase, doorstep, balustrade, archway, bridge and vaulted ceiling you will ever draw, and it is still, literally and unchanged, the grid walk from section 5. Nothing has been added except a willingness to keep going, and a pair of integers.

It also has a property worth appreciating. Because rows leave the window the instant something covers them, every pixel is written exactly once, in order from nearest to furthest. No pixel is ever painted over. There is no depth buffer, no sorting, and no wasted work anywhere in the drawing of the world. Very few renderers of any kind can say that.

Do this bit even though it looks pointless

Notice that the loop calls sink.floor(...) and sink.wall(...) rather than writing pixels itself. All it hands over is: which rows, how far away, and which surface. Something else entirely decides what that means.

Right now that indirection buys you nothing and costs nothing. Later it is the difference between an afternoon and a rewrite. It is what lets the very same walk feed a pixel-by-pixel filler on the CPU today, and a graphics card that draws each run of rows as one small quad tomorrow (section 16), with the two guaranteed to agree about the shape of the world because there is only one copy of the code that knows about it.

Walking on it

The player has to agree with the renderer, or you will be able to see a step you cannot climb, which players report as a bug with impressive speed. In try_move from section 7, replace “is the target tile solid?” with three questions:

  • Is there room to stand? The gap ch - fh has to be taller than you are.
  • Is the step up small enough to climb? Compare the target floor with the one you are standing on. About 0.4 of a tile is a good limit. Anything taller is a wall as far as your legs are concerned, even though the renderer will happily draw it as a step.
  • Are you about to hit your head? The target tile's ceiling must clear your eye height.

Then set cam.z to the floor height of the tile you are standing on plus your eye height, but ease it there over a few frames rather than snapping. Moving a camera vertically by a whole step in a single frame is genuinely unpleasant to look at.

Summary
  • A tile carries a floor height and a ceiling height. Solid means the ceiling is not above the floor.
  • The ray keeps marching past obstructions, maintaining an unpainted band [ytop, ybot) which only ever shrinks.
  • A higher floor next door emits a riser; a lower ceiling emits a lintel; a solid tile fills what remains and ends the walk.
  • Every pixel is written exactly once, front to back. No depth buffer, no sorting.

12Going outside

The sky is the only thing in the world that is infinitely far away, which makes drawing it largely a matter of not doing something.

Mark some tiles as open air. When the march is inside one of those, it simply skips the ceiling step: it leaves the top of the clip window open and paints nothing there. Since the sky is further away than everything, anything the ray meets later is nearer and paints over those rows quite correctly. Whatever rows are still open when the march finishes are the rows that nothing covered, and those are your sky.

There is one complication, and it is a good illustration of how this style of engine thinks. If an open tile sits next to a solid block, how tall should that block be drawn? A solid tile fills the clip window all the way to the top of the screen, which was the rule in section 11, and that would mean no sky above any wall anywhere. Your horizon would be a continuous wall of stone.

The fix is the one Doom used. On an open-air tile the ceiling number has no job to do, because there is no ceiling. So give it a different one: let it mean the skyline, the height at which the mass next door stops being wall and starts being sky. One number, reused, and no new machinery anywhere.

That has a consequence which shapes how you build every outdoor scene, so it is worth stating plainly: outdoors, build with raised floors, never with solid tiles. A solid tile can never have sky above it, so anything built out of solid tiles is a hole punched in your sky.

Once you accept that, it turns out to be liberating rather than restrictive, because a raised floor is a considerably more interesting object than a wall:

  • A castle wall is a tile whose floor is at 4.6.
  • A battlement is a row of tiles alternating between 6.0 and 5.0, which reads as a wall from the courtyard below and as a parapet you can see over from the walkway behind it. Same tiles, two meanings, depending only on where you are standing.
  • A well is a tile whose floor is at −1.7.

The renderer knows none of those words. It draws all three with the same “the next floor is higher, so show a wall face” rule it uses for a doorstep.

Drawing the sky itself

Bake a wide panorama once at start-up, in which the horizontal axis is compass bearing and the vertical axis is elevation. A screen column then maps to exactly one column of the panorama, which means the atan2 that works out the bearing happens once per column rather than once per pixel.

Make the vertical axis linear in tan(elevation) rather than in the elevation angle itself. That sounds like an odd choice until you notice that tan(elevation) is exactly what the projection already hands you, since rows of the screen are evenly spaced in that quantity and not in angle. The per-pixel cost then drops to a multiply and a load.

Summary
  • Open-air tiles simply skip the ceiling step, leaving the top of the clip window unpainted. Whatever remains open at the end is sky.
  • Reuse the ceiling height on an open tile to mean the skyline: the height at which neighbouring walls stop.
  • Build outdoor scenery from raised floors rather than solid tiles.

13Slopes

One height per tile means every hillside is a staircase. Give each tile four heights, one per corner, and the staircase becomes a landscape. This is the most mathematical section in the article, and it still comes down to solving one quadratic.

Everything so far treats a tile's floor as a flat plate held at one height. For a flagstone room that is exactly right. For a landscape it is exactly wrong, and the reason is worth seeing clearly.

Terrain is written one number per tile: this square is at 1.2, its neighbour at 1.5. Each square is level, so the join between them is a vertical face 0.3 tall. Do that across a whole map and you get a world quantised in whatever units it happens to be stored in. A hill you can walk up reads as a flight of stairs, a bank beside a road reads as a kerb, and a pitched roof reads as a wedding cake.

The fix is to stop asking “how high is this tile?” and start asking “how high is each corner of it?” Four numbers instead of one:

Listing 18. A tile with a shapefour corners instead of one plate
pub struct Tile {
    pub fh: f32,          // floor height at the centre
    pub ch: f32,          // ceiling, or skyline outdoors
    pub slope: [f32; 4],  // corner offsets from fh: NW, NE, SW, SE
    // ... textures and flags as before
}

// The surface between the corners is the bilinear patch. Offsets that sum
// to zero keep `fh` meaning exactly what it always meant, the height in
// the middle, so every existing reader of it is still correct.
impl Tile {
    pub fn floor_uv(&self, u: f32, v: f32) -> f32 {
        let s = self.slope;
        self.fh + lerp(lerp(s[0], s[1], u), lerp(s[2], s[3], u), v)
    }
}

What happens between those four corners? The obvious answer is the right one: mix them. Slide partway across the tile and take that fraction of the way between the two left corners and the two right ones, then do the same again from front to back. Mixing in two directions like that is called bilinear interpolation, and it is the same operation you would use to fade between four colours. Here it fades between four heights. The shape it produces is a gently warped sheet, rather like a handkerchief pinned at its corners.

Two things about that sheet are the whole reason this is affordable rather than a research project.

  • Along an edge, it is just a straight line between that edge's two corners. The two corners on the far side of the tile get no say at all. So if two neighbouring tiles are given the same numbers for the corners they share, their surfaces meet exactly, all the way along the join, with no crack and no step, whatever either of them is doing elsewhere. That single fact is what makes seamless terrain possible without the renderer knowing anything whatsoever about terrain.
  • Along a ray, it is a quadratic, which is to say a curve of the shape a·x² + b·x + c, the sort you learned to solve at school. That means finding where a ray meets the ground is not a search and not a march. It is one root of one quadratic, computed directly.

One root per pixel

Here is the whole calculation, and it is considerably shorter than the explanation of it. Two facts, set equal to each other.

What the screen row tells you. From section 9, a row of the screen only ever shows surfaces lying on one particular sloping line going away from your eye: closer things appear lower down the window, further things nearer the horizon. Writing s for how steep that line is for row y, whatever that row shows must be at height pz - s·d, where pz is your eye height and d the distance. For a level floor the height is a fixed number, so you can rearrange that into the single division you already wrote in section 9.

What the ground says. Its height changes as you travel along the ray, and by the second property above, it changes as a quadratic.

The pixel is where those two agree, so set them equal to each other:

row says: h = pz − s·d surface says: h = p + q·e + r·e² e = d − d0 so: r·e² + (q + s)·e + (p − pz + s·d0) = 0
p, q, r
describe the ground along this one ray: its height where the ray enters the cell, how fast that height is changing, and how much that rate itself changes. Three multiply-adds, worked out once for the whole run of rows and not per pixel.
s
the steepness of the line of sight for this screen row.
d0
the distance at which the ray entered this cell.
e
the distance we are solving for, measured from the cell entry rather than from the camera.

That last choice is not tidiness. Measured from the camera, those three coefficients become large numbers that very nearly cancel each other out, and in ordinary 32-bit arithmetic a hillside at the far end of the view comes out visibly terraced by the very sums that were supposed to smooth it. Measuring from the cell entry keeps every quantity small and the arithmetic well behaved.

Now solve it, but not with the formula you memorised at school. On gentle ground r is very small, and the schoolbook (-b ± √disc) / 2a handles that badly. The top of the fraction becomes one number minus another almost identical number, which throws away most of your significant digits, and when r reaches exactly zero it divides by zero rather than politely turning back into the flat case. There is an algebraically equivalent form that behaves itself, and it is no longer:

Listing 19. Solving it safelythis is the whole of slope rendering
pub fn plane_root(r: f32, b: f32, c: f32) -> f32 {
    if r == 0.0 { return -c / b; }              // level: the classic floor cast
    let disc = b * b - 4.0 * r * c;
    if disc <= 0.0 { return -c / b; }           // ray runs along the surface
    let qh = -0.5 * (b + b.signum() * disc.sqrt());
    c / qh                                      // tends to -c/b as r tends to 0
}

Keep the flat case as its own branch, as the first line does. Most of any map, every room, every ceiling and every block of masonry, is still perfectly level, and there is no reason for any of it to pay for a square root. The test is on a value that does not change down the whole run of rows, so the processor's branch predictor gets it right every single time and it effectively costs nothing.

Three edits to the march

So what does all this do to the loop from section 11? Remarkably little. Everywhere it used to read a tile's one floor height, it now asks the tile how high it is at a particular point. Three places change:

  1. Where the floor span starts. The silhouette of a floor is where the surface stands at the far end of the run: its own height on a level tile, or the height under the exact point the ray leaves the cell on a sloping one.
  2. Whether there is a riser at all. Compare the two tiles' floors at the seam itself, which is the only place they can meaningfully be compared. Where the corners agree, the two heights are equal at every point along that edge, so no riser is emitted, and terrain becomes seamless without the renderer ever being told that a hillside is a thing.
  3. How the pixel finds its distance. The root above, in place of the division.
Listing 20. The boundary testnow asking about a point, not a tile
// Both floors at the crossing point, not at the two tile centres.
let cur_h  = cur.floor_on(curx, cury, hx, hy);
let next_h = next.floor_on(mapx, mapy, hx, hy);

if next_h > cur_h + 1e-4 {                       // a step up: a riser
    let ybeg = row_at(next_h, inv_d, f).max(ytop);
    if ybeg < ybot { sink.wall(ybeg, ybot, &span); }
    ybot = ybot.min(ybeg);
}

The small 1e-4 tolerance in that comparison is doing real work. Two surfaces that are meant to be at the same height will often differ in the last bit or two of their floating-point representation, and without the tolerance you would emit a wall face zero pixels tall at random along every seam, which flickers.

Floors above your head

One old assumption has to go, and it is the interesting one. A level floor is only ever seen from above, so the march has always skipped any tile whose floor is over your head. That is perfectly sensible: you cannot see the top of a table you are standing underneath.

A sloping floor, though, has no single side. The near half of a bank can be under your feet while its far half is above your eye. And a pitched roof is the extreme case: it is a floor you are looking at entirely from below.

You can see a roof for exactly the same reason you can see a hillside: it tilts up and towards you, so its surface faces you. Whether it is visible from where you happen to be standing has a pleasingly simple answer. Call the eaves height E, your eye height z, the pitch m (how much the roof climbs for each unit you go back), and d the distance to the eaves. You can see the roof as soon as:

m · d > E − z

In words: the roof rises above the eaves faster than the eaves rise above your eye, so stand back far enough and the roof appears from behind its own guttering. Which is exactly what happens in a real street.

Authoring it

You now have a renderer that can draw a slope, and a level editor that would be horrible to use, because nobody wants to type in four corner heights per tile. Do not make whoever builds your world think in corners. Writing terrain as one number per tile is the only sane way to write it, so keep every bit of that, and add a single pass at the very end of world building that turns the tile heights into corner heights.

The trick is to work out the corners first, as a grid of their own, and then hand each tile its four. Because both tiles either side of a join read the same entry from that grid, they cannot possibly disagree. The seamlessness comes for free, rather than from any smoothing or fixing-up afterwards:

Listing 21. Tile heights to corner heightsone pass, at the very end of world building
// One lattice point per cell corner: the mean of the four cells meeting there.
for cy in 0..=MH { for cx in 0..=MW {
    let four = [(cx-1, cy-1), (cx, cy-1), (cx-1, cy), (cx, cy)];
    // Only ordinary walkable ground of the same kind, and only where the
    // four stand close enough together to be one surface.
    if four.iter().all(smoothable) && spread(four) <= max_step {
        corner[cy * CW + cx] = mean(four);
    }
}}
// Then hand every cell its four samples. Where a lattice point said nothing,
// the cell keeps its own height there, which is exactly a vertical step.

What that pass refuses to touch matters as much as what it smooths, and this is the part to get right. Skip any corner where one of the four tiles is solid, is a block of masonry, is under water, or has a wall standing in it. Skip it if the four are not all indoors or all outdoors, so that a doorway threshold does not tilt. And skip it if the four disagree by more than a single step, because that is not a slope, it is a cliff.

Every one of those refusals leaves the tiles keeping their own heights at that corner, which is precisely the old behaviour: a clean vertical face. So cliffs stay cliffs, a quay keeps its edge, and the waterline stays a line. Smoothing everything is what makes a world look like melted wax. The judgement is entirely in the exceptions.

Mark the made things

Add a flag meaning “this floor was cut by hand” and set it on stair treads, ramps and bridge decks. Left to itself, the pass finds a flight of steps a quarter of a unit apart, decides it is a hillside, and hands your village a slipway. A deck is level because somebody built it level, and a tread is a tread precisely because you can see the riser.

Roofs come out of the same machinery from the other direction, and this is the payoff for buildings. Take a rectangle, and work out how far in from the nearest edge each corner of the grid is. That many steps up the pitch is its height. Feed those to the tiles and a stack of blocks becomes a plane:

Listing 22. A hipped roofmeasure the pitch at the corners, not the centres
let at = |gx: i32, gy: i32| {
    // How far in from the nearest eaves this lattice point stands.
    let inset = (gx - x0).min(x1 + 1 - gx).min(gy - y0).min(y1 + 1 - gy).max(0);
    eaves + inset as f32 * pitch
};
for y in y0..=y1 { for x in x0..=x1 {
    self.block_sloped(x, y, [at(x, y), at(x+1, y), at(x, y+1), at(x+1, y+1)], wall, cover);
}}

Because neighbouring tiles are handed the same corner numbers, the hips and the ridge arrive as creases in one continuous surface rather than as the little vertical steps between a hundred terraces. And because the outermost ring of corners sits at exactly the eaves height, the wall underneath stays a level line all the way round the building, however the roof behind it climbs.

Walking on it

The collision test changes in the same way the renderer did: ask how high the ground is under your foot, not how high the tile is. It matters more than it sounds. A step too tall to climb at the far side of a sloping tile may be perfectly climbable at the near side, and measuring at the middle gets both answers wrong.

Make that one substitution and a smoothed hillside becomes something you walk up smoothly and continuously, while the step-height rule from section 11 carries on doing its job in all the places that really are steps.

Summary
  • Store four corner heights per tile and interpolate bilinearly between them.
  • Along a shared edge the surface depends only on the two shared corners, so tiles given the same corner numbers meet seamlessly.
  • Along a ray the surface is a quadratic, so each pixel's distance is one root, solved directly. Use the numerically stable form and keep the flat case as a fast branch.
  • Author terrain as one height per tile and derive the corners in a final pass, refusing to smooth across cliffs, walls, water and anything built by hand.

14Sprites

A barrel is a flat picture that quietly turns to face you wherever you stand. Drawing one is easy. Deciding which of its pixels are hidden behind things is the actual work.

These are usually called billboards: a rectangle of image, always square-on to the camera, standing in the world. Because it always faces you, you never need more than one picture of it, and because it is flat, it costs about as much to draw as a wall.

Where on the screen, and how big

To place a sprite you need to know two things: how far ahead of you it is, and how far to the side. You already have the two arrows that answer exactly those questions, dir and plane from section 4. Take the offset from you to the sprite, and measure it against each of them.

Measuring a vector against two other vectors that are not the usual x and y axes means solving a small two-by-two system, which is four multiplies, a subtraction and a division. The division is by the determinant of the pair, which is a number that depends only on the camera and can therefore be computed once per frame rather than once per sprite.

Listing 23. Placing a billboardwhere on screen, and how big
let ox = spr.x - cam.x;      // offset from the camera to the sprite
let oy = spr.y - cam.y;
let inv_det = 1.0 / (planex * diry - dirx * planey);   // once per frame

let tx = inv_det * ( diry * ox - dirx * oy);     // sideways offset, in plane units
let ty = inv_det * (-planey * ox + planex * oy); // depth, in the same units as the DDA
if ty < 0.08 { continue; }                       // behind the camera, or on top of it

let inv = 1.0 / ty;
let sx  = W as f32 * 0.5 * (1.0 + tx * inv);   // centre column on screen
let hw  = 0.5 * spr.w * focal * inv;           // half width in pixels
let y1  = horizon as f32 - (spr.z - cam.z) * focal * inv;          // feet
let y0  = horizon as f32 - (spr.z + spr.h - cam.z) * focal * inv;  // head

Everything after the division is the same “further is smaller” projection as every other section: a width in the world times focal / ty gives a width in pixels, and a height above the eye times focal / ty gives an offset in rows from the horizon. The last two lines are row_at from section 10 written out longhand, and if you would rather call row_at, do.

The line that matters most is ty. It comes out as the same kind of distance the grid walk produces, perpendicular and in the same units, so you can compare it directly against wall distances. Get that wrong and your sprites will sink into walls near the edges of the screen while looking perfectly fine in the middle, which is a memorably annoying way to spend an evening.

Hiding things behind other things

Now the hard part. A sprite has to disappear behind a pillar, and the pillar has already been drawn by the time we get here.

As you fill in the world, write each pixel's distance into a second array the same size as the screen: a depth buffer. Then sort your sprites furthest-first, and when you paint one, skip any pixel whose stored distance is nearer than the sprite's. A barrel behind a pillar then disappears behind it one pixel at a time, without you ever having to think about it.

The classic trick is to store one distance per column rather than per pixel, which is a few hundred times smaller. It works perfectly in a world of full-height walls, because a whole column of the sprite really is either in front of the wall or behind it. But the moment you have the varying floor heights of section 11, you will want a barrel cut off halfway up by a step, and a statue cut off at the waist by a railing. That needs a distance per pixel.

Two last details. Sprites need transparency, so pick a colour to mean “invisible” or keep an alpha channel, and simply skip those texels. And things that glow, such as flames, sparks and lamps, look far better added to what is behind them rather than replacing it. Draw those last, adding their colour in, and do not let them write into the depth buffer, so that several can pile up on top of one another.

Characters, and the eight-way trick

A barrel looks the same from every side. A person does not, and drawing one properly would mean a 3D model, which we have carefully avoided owning. The traditional answer is to draw the character from eight directions in advance and pick whichever one matches.

The subtlety is which direction to choose. Pick it using the character's facing measured against your line of sight to them, not against your own facing. Use your own facing and the character appears to pivot on the spot as you sidestep past, which is unsettling in a way players notice immediately without being able to say why.

Advance the walk animation by distance travelled rather than by elapsed time, too. Then the feet cannot skate along the ground however you later tune the walking speed.

Summary
  • Measure the offset to the sprite against dir and plane to get depth and sideways position. The depth comes out in the same units as the wall distances.
  • Project width and height with the same focal / distance rule as everything else.
  • Keep a per-pixel depth buffer and draw sprites furthest-first. Per-column depth is not enough once floors vary in height.
  • For characters, pre-render eight facings and choose between them relative to the line of sight.

15Light and fog

The simple “further away is darker” from section 8 gets you surprisingly far. Real lamps and torches need one more idea: work the lighting out before the game starts, and look it up.

Fog first

Fog is the cheapest large improvement available, and it also does a structural job. Your renderer gives up at some maximum distance, because it has to, and without fog you can see that giving-up as a hard edge where the world simply stops. If the fog becomes completely opaque at exactly that distance, the edge becomes invisible: there was nothing to see out there anyway, and nobody has to know why.

Mechanically it is a blend of each pixel towards a fog colour by an amount that depends on nothing but distance. Anything that depends on exactly one number can be worked out in advance and stored in an array, which is called a lookup table, so that you never evaluate the curve itself while drawing. You will do this again and again in section 16.

Listing 24. The fog tablebuilt once at start-up
// 640 entries at 16 per world unit covers the 34-unit far clip.
let fog: Vec<u32> = (0..640).map(|i| {
    let d = i as f32 / 16.0;
    let t = 1.0 - (-(d * 0.058).powf(1.8)).exp();
    // Force it fully opaque by the clip plane, or you can see the edge.
    let t = t.max(smoothstep(34.0 - 7.0, 34.0, d));
    (t.clamp(0.0, 1.0) * 256.0) as u32     // 8.8 fixed point
}).collect();

The stored value is the blend factor scaled by 256, so that mixing can be done in whole numbers: multiply, then shift right by 8. That is the fixed-point trick which section 16 recommends for the inner loops, appearing here for the first time.

A baked lightmap

Working out what fifty torches contribute to a pixel, sixty times a second, is hopeless. But here is the thing: the level does not move. So work it out once, at start-up, and store the answer. That stored answer is a lightmap.

Make a 2D grid a few times finer than the tile map, since eight of these per tile in each direction works well, and for each point on it add up what every light contributes:

  1. Attenuate by distance from the light, so that near things are brighter.
  2. Fire a shadow ray from the point to the light, using the very same grid walk you wrote in section 5. You are simply asking it a different question. If it crosses a tile whose floor rises above the ray, or whose ceiling drops below it, then something is in the way and this point is in shadow.
  3. Blur the result slightly. One shadow ray gives a hard, jagged shadow edge, and a light blur turns it into something that looks like firelight. Then fill in any point that landed inside a wall, so that when you sample smoothly near a wall you do not pick up a black hole and get a dark seam along the bottom of it.

While drawing, all this costs you is one smooth sample of that grid at the world position of the pixel, and you already worked that position out for the floor cast in section 9. For a wall, sample just in front of its face rather than inside it, or you will be asking what the light is like in the middle of a block of stone. It is dark in there.

Two refinements make a flat, two-dimensional lightmap look convincingly three-dimensional.

First, store alongside the colour the number of the brightest light at that point. A single torch can then flicker while you play, by multiplying by one per-light factor looked up from a small array, so that one guttering torch dims its own corridor and nothing else.

Second, dim the light with height above the floor, since torches sit about a metre up. That single line is what keeps a corridor evenly lit while the ceiling of a tall hall falls away into darkness, and it is a remarkably large improvement for a remarkably small amount of code.

Do not clamp

One last trap, and it is a good one to know about generally. When light multiplies a colour past full brightness, the obvious fix is to cut each of red, green and blue off at the maximum. Do not.

Warm stone under a bright torch has a lot of red and less blue. Red therefore hits the ceiling first and stays there while green carries on climbing, so the colour slides towards a flat yellow-green as it gets brighter, and your torchlit corridor develops an unpleasant radioactive quality. Instead, push the value through a curve that is straight up to about 0.72 and then bends gently towards the maximum without ever quite reaching it. Bright things then desaturate smoothly towards white, which is what film does and what your eye expects. Tabulate the curve and it costs one lookup.

Summary
  • Fog is a distance-only blend, so it is a lookup table. Make it fully opaque at the far clip distance to hide the edge of the world.
  • Bake static lighting into a grid finer than the tile map, using the section 5 grid walk for shadow rays.
  • Store the brightest light's index per sample so that individual lights can flicker at run time.
  • Roll bright colours off through a curve rather than clamping each channel independently.

Part III · Speed and finishing

16Making it fast

Everything above was written to be understood. Here is what to change once it works, roughly in order of how much you get back per line you touch.

Do not do any of this until the picture is right. Optimisation is where renderers quietly break, and a renderer that is wrong and fast is very much harder to fix than one that is wrong and slow.

Table 3. Optimisations, in the order worth attempting them.
ChangeWhy it pays
Lower internal resolutionRender at a half or a third of the window size and scale up. Costs one integer upscale and immediately halves or thirds the whole per-pixel budget. Do this first. It is also period-correct.
Column-major buffersVertical stripes are the unit of work, so make a column contiguous in memory: both the framebuffer and the textures. Then transpose once at the end, when you present the frame.
Tables instead of arithmeticAny curve that depends on exactly one number can be precomputed into an array: 1/(y - horizon), the fog, the tone curve, the light falloff, the flicker. Pinning the horizon to a whole row back in section 10 is what makes the first of those an exact table rather than an approximation.
MipmapsA distant wall covers few pixels, so it samples its texture at every fourth or eighth texel, which sparkles horribly as you move and reads scattered memory besides. Keep shrunken copies of every texture (64, 32, 16, 8) and pick one based on how many texels a pixel covers. If you can afford it, keep the fractional level and blend between two of them, or you will see a visible pop as you walk towards things.
Fixed pointDo the light and colour blending in whole numbers rather than decimals: store 1.0 as 256, multiply, then shift right by 8 to get back. This is how it was done at the time, and it is still a win in the innermost loop.
ThreadsColumns do not depend on one another at all, so hand a slice of them to each core. With column-major buffers each thread owns its own separate run of memory and needs no locks whatsoever. A dozen cores gets you close to a dozen times the speed, which is rare enough in this line of work to be worth enjoying.
Hand the spans to a GPUThe endgame, and the rest of this section. Keep the DDA on the CPU, record each span as a dozen floats, and draw them as instanced one-pixel-wide quads with the shading in a fragment shader.

Handing the spans to a GPU

The last row of that table deserves a section of its own, because the interesting thing about it is that it is not a rewrite. Almost everything you have written stays exactly where it is.

Start by naming the thing we are moving. A span is a run of rows in one column, all covered by the same surface: rows 40 to 96 of column 312 are the floor of tile (7, 4), seen at such and such a distance. Your renderer already thinks in these. Every sink.floor(...) and sink.wall(...) call in section 11 is a span. A whole frame is a few thousand of them.

The plan: the grid walk stays on the CPU and carries on deciding which span covers what. Instead of filling those spans in with pixels, it writes each one down as a dozen numbers. Then the graphics card draws all of them at once, and the shading, meaning the texture lookups, the lighting and the fog, happens there.

Leaving the walk on the CPU is a deliberate choice rather than a limitation. It is inherently one-thing-after-another down a column, since each cell's clip window depends on the one before it; it is cheap; and above all it is the only copy of the rules your world obeys. Keeping one copy means the two versions of your renderer can differ in how things are shaded but never in what is where.

A span is twelve floats

So the pixel-filling implementation of the sink is joined by a second one that just appends numbers to an array, one array per kind of span. Twelve floats each:

Listing 25. Recording one wall spaninstead of painting it
self.b.wall.extend_from_slice(&[
    c.x as f32, y0 as f32, y1 as f32, s.d,          // where on screen, how far away
    s.u, s.tex as f32, s.floor_h, s.outdoor,        // which texel column, which material
    light.r, light.g, light.b, 0.0,                 // lightmap fetch, done once
]);

That third line is a principle in miniature, and it is worth stating on its own: anything that is the same all the way down a span should be worked out on the CPU. A wall slice stands at one distance, on one spot of floor, facing one direction, so its lightmap sample, its flicker and its shading can all be computed once per span and travel as three numbers. A floor span cannot do that, because its world position moves under every single pixel, so its shader looks the lighting up itself. Divide the work by what actually varies and the amount you send stays tiny.

Almost nothing else is sent per frame. Your textures go across once at start-up, as a stack of images the card can index into by number, which means no gaps to leak, no neighbours bleeding into each other, and nothing to swap over between draws. The lightmap goes once. The camera settings are a handful of numbers. Per frame, the only traffic is the list of spans.

No vertex buffer at all

Here is a trick worth knowing well beyond this project. A span is a rectangle one pixel wide, which needs four corners, but you do not have to send any corners. Every shader invocation already knows which of the four it is, from gl_VertexID, and the span record already says which column and which rows. So the corner is two lines of arithmetic:

Listing 26. The whole vertex stageno vertex data at all
float fx = aA.x + float(gl_VertexID & 1);            // left or right edge
float fy = (gl_VertexID < 2) ? aA.y : aA.z;          // top or bottom row

// Walls have one distance for the whole span, so depth can be settled here.
// Planes cannot: theirs changes under every pixel.
float z = mix(1.0, clamp(aA.w / MAX_DIST, 0.0, 1.0) * 2.0 - 1.0, uSpanDepth);
gl_Position = vec4(fx * uSize.z * 2.0 - 1.0, 1.0 - fy * uSize.w * 2.0, z, 1.0);

Then you ask the card to draw that four-cornered shape n times over, once per span, in a single call, with one call per kind of span. Five calls for the entire world, whatever happens to be in it.

Depth without sorting

Normally a GPU works out what is in front of what by comparing depths as it draws. You do not need any of that. Your grid walk already guarantees that each pixel is covered exactly once, so turn the depth test off entirely, while leaving depth writing on, purely so that the billboards drawn afterwards have something to compare themselves against.

That also explains the uSpanDepth line above. A wall span sits at one distance, so its depth can be decided once per corner, in the cheap stage. A floor span's depth changes under every pixel, so it has to be written per pixel, which is slower. Using separate programs for the two means that only the floors pay for it.

Uploading without stalling

One trap, and it is the classic way to accidentally make a GPU slower than a CPU. You rewrite the span list every frame. If you overwrite the memory the card is holding, the driver has no choice but to stop and wait until last frame's drawing has finished reading it, and now your fast path is a queue.

The fix is one word: instead of writing into the existing buffer, hand over a whole new one of the same name. In OpenGL, that means calling glBufferData rather than glBufferSubData. The driver quietly sets the old memory aside to be freed when it has finished with it, and gives you fresh memory immediately. No waiting, and no juggling two buffers by hand.

While you are here: collecting spans parallelises perfectly. Give each thread a band of columns and its own list to append to, so that nothing is shared and no locks are needed, then join the lists end to end at the finish.

What gets better, not just faster

Table 4. Things the GPU path can do that the CPU path cannot afford.
On the GPUWhat changes
Mip selectionKeep the fractional level and let the hardware blend between two mips. The CPU path cannot afford the second fetch, so distant stone pops as you walk towards it. Here it does not.

Post-processingBright pass, four separable blurs at quarter resolution, composite. On the CPU that is a serious fraction of the frame; here it is a few full-screen triangles.

The one discipline to keep is that your two renderers must go on agreeing. Every constant that shapes the picture, such as the fog density, the knee of the tone curve and the light scale, should exist in exactly one place, and the shader source should be assembled at start-up with those values pasted into it. Tune a curve and both change together. Skip this and the CPU version slowly rots into something you no longer trust, which is a shame, because it is the reference that tells you which of the two is wrong.

For scale: at 1920 by 1080, with the same world drawn both ways, this project takes around 26 ms a frame across twelve CPU cores, against around 1 ms on a mid-range graphics card. In that second case, most of the CPU's remaining time is the grid walk that both of them share.

Keep yourself honest

Two habits make all of the above safe, and both take about an hour to build.

First, a screenshot mode: a flag that puts the camera at a dozen fixed positions, renders each one to an image file, and exits. Now “did that change break anything?” is a file comparison rather than a judgement call, and you will trust your own optimisations a great deal more.

Second, a sweep mode: put the camera on every walkable tile in the map, facing four ways, and render the lot. Nobody ever looks at the output. The point is that it visits every material, every distance and every span type in your world, so if you run it in a debug build, with array bounds and integer overflow checks switched on, it will find the indexing bug that a release build silently wraps straight past.

Summary
  • Cut the internal resolution first, then make everything column-major, then replace one-argument curves with tables.
  • Columns are independent, so threading is nearly free.
  • Moving to a GPU means recording spans rather than filling them. The grid walk itself stays on the CPU, as the single source of truth about the world.
  • Build a screenshot mode before you optimise anything, not afterwards.

17When it goes wrong

Raycaster bugs are unusually well behaved: each one has a distinctive look, and the look tells you which line to go and stare at.

This is the most useful table in the article, and it is worth reading once now even if everything currently works, because recognising a symptom is very much faster than bisecting your own code at midnight.

Table 5. Symptoms and their usual causes.
What you seeWhat it usually is
Flat walls bulge towards you in the centre of the screen You are using the distance the ray actually travelled instead of the perpendicular distance. See the end of section 5. If you built your rays from angles rather than from a camera plane, you need the cos correction.
Walls bulge away from you, pinched in the middle The correction has been applied twice: once by the camera plane and once by hand. Remove the manual cosine.
The whole image is one flat colour The camera is inside a wall, so every ray hits at distance zero. Check your starting position against the map, remembering that the map's first row is y = 0.
The view shears or swims as you turn plane is not exactly at right angles to dir, or one of the two was computed from a stale copy of the angle. Recompute both together, once per frame, from the same sin_cos.
The world is mirrored left to right camx runs the wrong way, or plane points to your left. Flip one sign, not both.
Textures read correctly on some walls and mirrored on others The u flip in section 8 is missing or applied to the wrong pair of faces.
The texture slides vertically as you walk towards a wall tv was started from the clamped top of the stripe rather than the unclamped one, so the offset is lost exactly when the stripe overflows the window.
Occasional crash, or garbage pixels, when very close to a wall line_h is not clamped before being used as an array index. See section 6.
Every wall is one cell too far away The distance is being recorded after crossing the boundary instead of before. Look at the order of the three statements in the march.
You can see through the corner of a pillar from one specific spot You are stepping along the ray in fixed increments rather than crossing boundaries. That is the failure DDA exists to eliminate.
The program hangs on some frames only A ray exactly parallel to an axis has produced an infinity or a NaN, so the comparison in the loop never becomes true. Check the 1e-8 guard, and keep the iteration cap.
Hairline cracks between surfaces, which flicker as you move Two surfaces that meet in the world were projected by two different routes. Route everything through row_at, as section 10 insists.
Distant walls sparkle or crawl as you move Texture undersampling. You need mipmaps: section 16.
Sprites sink into walls near the edges of the screen but look fine in the middle The sprite's depth is Euclidean while the wall's is perpendicular, so the two are not comparable. Use ty from section 14.
Movement speed changes with the frame rate A missing dt on the keys, or a spurious one on the mouse. Both are common, and they produce opposite complaints.

Three ways to see what is happening

The general problem with debugging a renderer is that the output is a picture, and pictures are poor at explaining themselves. Three techniques help enormously, and all of them are worth building before you need them.

  • Draw the map. Put a small top-down view in a corner of the window showing the grid, your position, and every tenth ray drawn out to the point where it stopped. This is exactly what the panel at the top of this page does, and almost every geometry bug becomes obvious the moment you can see the rays. A ray that stops in the wrong place on the map is a cast bug; a ray that stops in the right place but draws wrongly is a projection bug. That single distinction saves hours.
  • Shrink the problem. Set W to 4 and print the four distances. Four numbers you can check by hand beat 320 stripes you cannot.
  • Colour by fact rather than by texture. Temporarily colour each wall stripe by side, or by (mapx + mapy) & 1, or by cell distance. Checkerboard colouring in particular makes the grid structure visible, so you can see immediately whether the walk is landing where you think it is.
Summary

Nearly every visual defect in this kind of renderer falls into one of three families: the wrong distance (bulging), the wrong convention (mirroring), or two pieces of code disagreeing about a boundary (cracks and flicker). Identify the family first, then the line.

18Where to go next

Each of these is a self-contained evening or two, and every one is built out of machinery you have already written.

  • Water. For each row of water, copy the pixel the same distance above the horizon as this one is below it, tint it blue, and nudge it sideways with a wobble table. That is a reflection, and it is convincing because the vanishing line of a flat pond really is the horizon. Read only from the column you are already drawing and it stays simple and thread-safe. Where the mirrored row falls off the top of the screen there is no pixel to copy, but if you have a sky you can ask the sky what colour it is up there instead, which is what puts the moon in the pond.
  • Textures from code. A little noise, added together at a few different scales, gives you stone, timber, grass and rust without a single image file. Being able to say “make this one mossier” by changing a number is a genuinely pleasant way to work. Smear the noise sideways and keep only the values near one threshold and you have lava: glowing seams between dark plates of crust.
  • Glow around bright things. Take a copy of your finished image, throw away everything that is not bright, shrink it to a quarter size, blur it, and add it back on top. Torches stop being orange blobs and start looking like light sources. Darkening the corners of the frame slightly, a vignette, is two lines and helps as much again.
  • Walls thinner than a tile. Everything here has assumed that a wall fills a whole square, which makes every building in your world about a metre and a half thick. Keep a list of line segments per tile instead, each with a thickness, and test the ray against them between the distances at which it enters and leaves the cell. Now a cottage has walls a foot thick, a doorway is something you walk through, and a fence can run at forty-five degrees across the grid.
  • People. The eight-way sprite trick from section 14, taken seriously: a walk cycle per direction, chosen against the line of sight, advanced by distance travelled.

A suggested order

If you are building this over several sittings, the order in this article is also the order that keeps you able to see what you have done. Every section ends in something visible, which matters more than it sounds: a renderer whose output you cannot look at is a renderer you cannot debug.

Three closing pieces of advice, all learned the hard way.

  • Keep one function that turns a height and a distance into a screen row (section 10) and route absolutely everything through it. Most visual bugs in an engine like this are two surfaces disagreeing by one pixel, and this is how you make that impossible rather than merely unlikely.
  • Keep the thing that decides what is where separate from the thing that colours it in (section 11). It costs nothing on the day, and it is what lets you swap in a completely different renderer later without touching a line of your world logic.
  • Write the screenshot mode early (section 16), even though it feels like a detour. The question “is this still correct?” comes up constantly, and you want it answered by a file comparison rather than by squinting.

The whole engine is one loop that walks a grid. Everything else in these eighteen sections, the heights, the sky, the slopes, the lights and the graphics card, is something you can add to that loop one piece at a time and see working the same afternoon. That is a rare quality in graphics programming, and it is why a technique older than most of the people using it is still one of the best ways to learn how any of this works.

Related techniques

  • Digital differential analyser. The same stepping idea is used to draw lines on a raster display, where it is usually met as Bresenham's algorithm. Grid traversal by boundary crossing is also the standard way to walk a voxel volume.
  • Portal rendering. The approach Doom and Duke Nukem 3D took instead: arbitrary convex rooms joined by openings, rather than a fixed grid. More flexible geometry, considerably more bookkeeping, and the clip window of section 11 is its close relative.
  • Voxel terrain. The heightmap renderers of the early 1990s cast one ray per column across a height field and drew it back to front. If you enjoyed section 13, that is the neighbouring valley.
  • Ray tracing. One ray per pixel rather than per column, with no assumption that the world is made of vertical walls. Far more general, and far more expensive.
